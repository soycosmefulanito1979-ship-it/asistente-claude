An Unfinished Love Story







Chapter 1 – A Line of Code, A Stroke of Chance

The sky over Buenos Aires hung low like a storm curtain waiting for a play that would never begin. The air smelled of old humidity, the kind that never leaves no matter how many windows you open. Every now and then the building trembled slightly as buses passed along the avenue below. It was 5:22 p.m., but it already looked like night.

Carlos sat down in front of his desk with his back slightly hunched, as if even his spine had grown tired of standing upright for other people. He was wearing a dark blue T-shirt with a small hummingbird printed over the chest. Worn out. The kind of shirt you never throw away even when it’s torn, because it still carries a scent that isn’t really a scent anymore, but memory. There was a dried coffee stain on the left knee of his gray sweatpants. He didn’t feel like changing. He had slept very little and eaten even less.

On the desk sat a black mug with the word “Compile” written across it in white letters. The coffee inside had gone cold. He didn’t know how long ago.

The room was dim, lit only by the glow of his laptop screen. The white walls showed patches of dampness gathering in the corners. A half-dead plant rested on the windowsill. A standing fan rotated with an intermittent clicking sound, as though it too was struggling to keep going.

And from the Bluetooth speaker, at a volume so low it sounded almost like a whisper trying not to interrupt, came a song as slow as it was sad. The song had appeared on its own, slipping quietly into the autoplay queue. Even so, he decided not to skip it. It wasn’t comforting. It hurt… but in exactly the right measure.

Carlos opened his code editor, that black screen filled with green letters and empty lines that always seemed to be waiting for something from him, but he didn’t type anything; he just stared at the blinking cursor for almost a full minute, as though time itself had stopped there, until, without thinking too much about it, he opened another browser tab instead. He wasn’t looking for anything in particular — not news, not memes, not even those cookie recipes he knew he would never actually make — and yet he somehow ended up inside a Facebook group, one of those places where people upload art without filters: films, photographs, badly cropped quotes, everything mixed together in a kind of chaotic refuge for sensitive people, broken people, creative people… or all three at once.

He scrolled lazily, almost out of inertia, not really connecting with anything, until the image appeared and stopped him cold. It was a painting with a red background crossed by blue stains that looked like half-healed wounds, with impulsive brushstrokes and thick lines that never fully formed a clear meaning; in the middle there was the suggestion of a figure, or maybe two, blurred and confused, as if they were embracing without knowing whether they should let go or remain exactly like that. Carlos stayed completely still, not because he couldn’t move, but because what he was looking at hurt in a way that was far too precise to ignore.

Underneath it, the caption read:

“Janet, from Montevideo. Oil painting. Untitled.”

Carlos swallowed hard. It felt as though someone had painted exactly the thing he himself had never known how to say.

Scroll. Comment box.

Without thinking too much about it, he typed:

“I don’t know why, but this made me feel something I can’t explain. Thank you for sharing it.”

He left the comment and kept scrolling as if nothing had happened, without stopping to think about what he had written or what might come afterward, without asking himself questions and, above all, without expecting absolutely anything.

He leaned back slightly in his chair, stretched his neck, and stared up at the ceiling where old cracks formed capricious shapes, like maps nobody had ever finished drawing. At that moment his phone vibrated, a brief interruption that broke the stillness; it was a Facebook Messenger notification, a private message. Carlos narrowed his eyes more out of habit than curiosity, because he wasn’t expecting absolutely anything, and yet he opened it anyway.

“Thank you. I’m Janet. I didn’t know someone else could feel the same thing I feel when looking at it.”

He read it once and then read it again, more slowly this time, as though he needed to confirm he hadn’t misunderstood; meanwhile, he began to feel a pressure in his chest that didn’t resemble his usual anxiety, nor surprise either, but something stranger, something harder to name, a feeling that wasn’t entirely emotion so much as the inevitable consequence of something that, deep down, he had already known.

The fan kept turning, the song had ended, but the silence left behind sounded exactly the same.

He rested his fingers on the keyboard.

Typed:

“Would it bother you if I told you I’ve spent more time thinking about that painting than about everything I was supposed to do today?”

She took a while to reply — not too long, but long enough for him to reach that slight discomfort of someone who, without wanting to admit it, is waiting. When the message finally appeared — “That happens to me every time I look at it too.” — Carlos smiled, faintly, but there was something different behind it now.

There was something there, something he didn’t fully understand yet, and he wasn’t even sure whether he wanted it to grow, but he did know that he could no longer pretend not to notice it. And so, inside a half-dark apartment, with an exhausted city pulsing outside and a song still lingering in the air as if it had no intention of leaving, something began. It wasn’t a love story, not yet, not even something that could be named easily; it was more like a different line of code slipping quietly into the middle of everything else, or a brushstroke that, without making a sound, ended up touching something deeper than the surface. And that, even without having a shape yet, was already enough.





Chapter 2 – Janet: Stains, Fears, and Messages (Janet’s POV)

The cold slipped in beneath the door with the persistence of a presence that never asks permission, and in Montevideo winter had that quiet way of settling into your bones without making a scene, without dramatics, simply staying. Janet sat on the floor with her legs crossed, her pants stained with oil paint like a record of everything she didn’t know how to say out loud; the Prussian blue had dried over her fingers and she hadn’t even noticed.

The studio was her refuge, but also the place where everything tightened inside her: the walls carried years of brushstrokes, failed attempts, silences that never completely disappeared. The window had been shut for weeks and the air held that dense mixture of turpentine, loneliness, and the faint lingering trace of her favorite perfume, the same one she used to wear back when she still felt like going out. On the wooden table, worn down by the constant friction of canvases and half-finished coffees, rested her phone, vibrating once without really disturbing the atmosphere; Janet was already used to those kinds of interruptions, small and almost irrelevant, the kind that rarely changed anything.

She picked it up with paint-stained fingers and saw the Facebook notification: a short comment, direct, unadorned.

“I don’t know why, but this made me feel something I can’t explain. Thank you for sharing it.”

She read it once and then again, not because she doubted what it said, but because there was something in the message that didn’t feel like the others. It wasn’t an empty compliment or one of those recycled phrases people throw around without thinking; there was a different kind of honesty in it, a kind of emotional nakedness that didn’t need to explain itself.

She stayed still for a moment, listening to her own breathing, noticing how the cold no longer felt entirely physical. The name was Carlos, a profile without a photo, a common last name, but the weight of the message wasn’t in the details — it was in what it suggested, in everything it left unsaid and still somehow managed to communicate. It felt like someone had understood something without her having to explain it, and instead of comforting her, it hurt a little.

Janet didn’t usually reply, especially not to strangers, but that day she was carrying a sadness heavier than usual: the argument with her mother still echoed inside her head, her grandmother still refused to eat, and she hadn’t sold a single painting all month. The feeling of being invisible had settled over her like a second skin. So, almost without thinking about it, she typed the way someone throws something into deep water without knowing whether it will float or sink.

“Thank you. I’m Janet. I didn’t know someone else could feel the same thing I feel when looking at it.”

When she pressed “Send,” she didn’t feel fear, but rather that strange certainty of having released something she could no longer take back. She stood up and walked toward the nearest chair, sitting sideways without fully settling into it, and looked again at the painting: the red background, the stains opened like wounds, the ambiguous shapes that could have been one figure or two, embracing without knowing whether that embrace was holding them together or breaking them apart a little more.

Her eyes filled with tears without warning, and it wasn’t a loud kind of crying, but the kind that hides inside breathing itself, as though taking a deeper breath might somehow loosen the knot inside her chest. She locked her phone screen, but something had already changed, a tiny crack opening inside the routine of stains and brushstrokes, an interference that hadn’t been there before. She didn’t know what it meant or where it was leading, only that it had appeared with the same naturalness certain things arrive with, the kind you never realize you needed until they’re already there.






Chapter 3 – Carlos: What We Love in Silence Hurts Too
The glow from the screen was the only thing truly lighting the room, while everything else remained wrapped in a warm dimness, the kind that doesn’t disturb you but never fully embraces you either. Carlos stared at his phone with an almost absurd persistence, as if something different might appear at any moment, though all that kept arriving were soulless notifications: promotions, irrelevant emails, some forwarded message from his mother. But not her. Not Janet.
Three days had passed since her last message and, even though they had never spoken about consistency or expectations or time, the absence was beginning to feel like a persistent echo, something bouncing around inside his head without ever fully fading away. Carlos wasn’t even sure what he was waiting for exactly — maybe a simple “hi,” some tiny sign, or perhaps just a silence that weighed a little less — but the waiting was there now, settled inside him without asking permission.
He let himself fall onto the couch without energy, noticing how the hummingbird T-shirt had lost its shape from overuse, as if even the fabric had grown tired of accompanying him through things. He held a mug in his hand, though he couldn’t really tell whether it was coffee or just lukewarm water carrying the ghost of what it used to be. On the screen, the cursor blinked steadily inside the chat window, marking time in a rhythm that seemed to mock his indecision; he didn’t type anything, only stared at it, as though that small flickering line could somehow translate everything he didn’t dare say out loud.
He closed his eyes for a moment and thought about her voice. He had never heard it, not once, and yet he had built it inside his head with unsettling precision, and that hurt more than he wanted to admit. He imagined it low but soft, the kind of voice that didn’t need to demand attention in order to stay with you. Janet’s silence wasn’t an ordinary absence; it had weight, it occupied space, it slipped into every corner like an uncomfortable presence made of doubt and waiting.
He took a deep breath, trying to lower the volume of what he was feeling, repeating to himself that he shouldn’t exaggerate, that they had barely begun talking, that it wasn’t love and couldn’t possibly be love. But the question kept finding its way in anyway: if it wasn’t that, then why did it hurt as if it already were? It wasn’t simple impatience. There was something deeper underneath it, a need that had nothing to do with the physical, as if some hidden part of him had recognized something of itself in her, something nobody else had ever touched before.
He stood up and walked toward the window, resting his eyes on the sky over Buenos Aires, gray and heavy without a single break in it, reflecting with uncomfortable precision everything he carried inside himself. And there, almost in a whisper he barely allowed himself to hear, he admitted it: he missed her. Not the way you miss someone who has left, but the way you miss someone who, somehow, has already become part of you and suddenly stops answering.
He went back to the laptop, opened a new unnamed document, and wrote a single sentence:
“Sometimes the silence of someone you care about feels like a scream nobody else can hear.”
He didn’t share it or send it anywhere, but simply writing it gave him a small sense of relief, as if he had at least managed to loosen part of the knot inside him.
The song changed and River kept playing, but not the way it had before. Now it sounded more like a whisper drifting dangerously close to the idea he had of her, of Janet, of this thing he still didn’t know how to name, but could no longer ignore.





Chapter 4 – Between Two Cities, the Same Waiting
That night, the sky over Buenos Aires and Montevideo wasn’t exactly the same, but there was something shared between them: a low mist stretching across both sides of the Río de la Plata, as though the water itself, still and heavy, reflected the same sleeplessness on either shore. The cities slept halfway, held together by orange streetlights, distant sirens, and lit windows where someone, somewhere, was also waiting for something that never quite arrived.
Carlos had turned off the computer more than an hour earlier, yet he was still sitting in the chair with his phone in his hand, staring at it as though it might suddenly offer him some direction that refused to appear. Janet’s last message was still there, brief but enough to keep echoing inside him:
“That’s love too, even if we don’t know who it belongs to.”
There had been no more words after that, though the silence didn’t feel empty or hostile, but full, like those spaces where the trace of someone still lingers even after they’re gone.
He thought about writing to her and about not doing it, then circled endlessly around the same thoughts he had already exhausted before finally falling still inside his own indecision. In the end he got up, walked to the kitchen, and came back with another coffee he didn’t need; he wasn’t sleepless, he was carrying absence, and that absence felt far too similar to insomnia.
On the other side of the river, Janet lay across her unmade bed with her face buried in the pillow and her arms wrapped around an old blanket she had kept since childhood. She wasn’t crying and she wasn’t sleeping either, only thinking, trapped inside a feeling she couldn’t tell was growing or merely surviving through inertia. She hadn’t painted in days and the canvases resting against the corner of the room seemed to watch her in silence, as though waiting for her to give them something she no longer knew how to offer. There was a change happening inside her, something she didn’t fully understand yet but could feel physically; when the heart falls out of rhythm, the hands begin to hesitate too.
She looked at her phone even knowing there were no notifications, unlocked it anyway, opened the chat, and reread their messages. She felt an unexpected tenderness mixed with a fear she couldn’t completely place. Carlos, sunk into his couch with his eyes lost on the dark screen in front of him, was moving through almost the exact same feeling, as if something invisible were synchronizing them despite the distance.
Two cities, two bodies, two separate heartbeats held together by the same waiting, by the same question neither of them dared to speak aloud: whether this thing happening between them was real. They didn’t say it or write it, but somehow, that night, they chose each other without needing to confirm it or give it a name.
The river still stretched between them, a screen could never replace an embrace, and doubt continued biting quietly at both of them, but even so, within that fragile and silent balance, they managed to feel each other. And for one brief but complete moment, that was enough.






Chapter 5 – The Ones Who Push When You’re Ready to Let Go
The café on the corner in Congreso still looked exactly the same as it always had, with its wide windows framed in aged wood, iron chairs bent out of shape by years of use, and a clock hanging on the wall that had shown the wrong time for years without anyone seeming to care. The soft clinking of teaspoons against ceramic cups blended with the constant movement of a waiter weaving calmly between tables, carrying himself with the kind of peace that belongs to people who are no longer chasing anything.
Carlos sat near the window wearing a worn black denim jacket, his shoelaces untied and the hummingbird T-shirt barely visible beneath the half-open zipper. The dark circles under his eyes weren’t from lack of sleep, but from everything he still couldn’t put in order inside his head. Across from him, Hernán ate a medialuna without any hurry, traces of plaster still dusted over his olive-green jacket and large work-worn hands; he had that rare way of listening without interrupting, which probably explained why they had remained friends since high school. Hernán never needed to understand every detail in order to stay present.
Carlos stirred his coffee absently, watching the foam spin in circles while he kept postponing whatever he wanted to say. From the speaker near the back of the café, I’ll Never Love Again drifted softly through the room, that broken voice slipping uninvited into all the places people tried hardest not to expose. Hernán watched him for a while, rested an elbow on the table, and finally asked without bothering to soften it first.
“Since when do you write poetry?”
Carlos blinked, caught off guard, as if he hadn’t expected someone to say out loud the very thing he himself avoided naming.
“What?”
“That thing you sent me the other day. The line about ‘thinking about someone quietly’… that’s poetry, man.”
Carlos shrugged awkwardly, running his fingers along the edge of the cup before finally answering.
“Since I started thinking about someone I can’t hold,” he said at last, his voice low, more honest than he would have liked.
Hernán didn’t laugh or make one of those easy jokes people use to escape uncomfortable conversations. If anything, he became more serious, as though he understood far more than Carlos assumed.
“Then don’t let her go.”
The sentence stayed suspended between them. Carlos lowered his eyes and hid the slight trembling in his lip behind another sip of coffee.
“I don’t know if you can let go of something you never really had.”
Hernán held his gaze steadily.
“Maybe you never had her physically, but if you talk about her like that, then she’s already inside you. And things like that don’t disappear easily.”
Carlos nodded slowly, letting the thought circle through his mind while the song continued playing, filling every corner of the café.
On the other side of the river, in Montevideo, the late afternoon light struggled to enter through the tall studio windows, as if even the air hesitated to cross into that suspended little world. Paintbrushes hung from clothespins, stained rags drooped like exhausted flags, and an electric kettle sat untouched since morning. Janet walked in small circles with her hair tied in a loose collapsing bun and an oversized denim shirt — her father’s — hanging over black pants stained with emerald green paint. She held a brush between her fingers the way someone holds a cigarette they never quite light.
Yoselin sat on the floor nearby, sorting paintbrushes by size for no real reason other than the calm that repetitive movements gave her.
“Carlos?” she asked without looking up.
Janet stopped walking, and even though she didn’t answer, the silence itself was enough confirmation.
“I thought you weren’t going to say anything,” Yoselin murmured, without judgment, only curiosity.
Janet let herself drop down beside her and slowly exhaled.
“I’m scared that this might be real,” she admitted, almost in a whisper, as though the sentence weighed more than she could comfortably carry.
Yoselin finally put the brush aside and looked at her.
“And what if it is?”
Janet swallowed hard, feeling something shift quietly inside her.
“I don’t know what to do with that. I’m not used to the things that make me feel good… staying.”
Yoselin turned toward her gently, with the kind of softness that didn’t need explanation.
“Then you stay, even if you’re scared. But don’t run away from the only thing that’s actually good for you.”
Janet closed her eyes, feeling the words pass through her while somehow holding her together at the same time.
“And what if it breaks me?”
“And what if it doesn’t?” Yoselin replied. “You always think good things come with an expiration date, but maybe this isn’t like before. Maybe this time someone stays.”
Janet rested her head against her friend’s shoulder and they remained there in silence, listening to the quiet creaking of the wooden floor and the distant murmur of the city slipping through the cracks in the windows. Then, from the forgotten phone resting on the table, as though it too wanted to participate in the moment, the same song began to play softly:
I’ll Never Love Again.
And in that instant, without needing to say it aloud, both of them understood that even though their lives were different, they were standing in front of the same thing: a moment where love still wasn’t a promise, but had already become a real possibility.





Carlos couldn’t sleep. The temperature said nothing — neither cold nor warm — and that neutral weather felt too similar to that uncertain point where you don’t know whether something is about to begin or quietly fall apart. He had turned the lights off a long time ago, but he was still sitting in front of the laptop, held together only by the glow of the screen, as though that alone were enough to keep him from completely collapsing.
The room was messy, not from carelessness but from lack of energy, and also because the disorder represented him better than any attempt to clean it ever could: a T-shirt thrown over the chair, a half-finished glass of water abandoned since who knows when, a crumpled printed page lying on the floor with lines of code that no longer meant anything, and the phone turned face down, as though avoiding the screen were easier than facing whatever it might bring.
The silence was almost complete, interrupted only by the steady ticking of the wall clock and by that other invisible noise, the one made by thoughts spinning endlessly without finding a way out. Carlos took a deep breath and returned, once again, to Janet’s last sentence:
“That’s love too, even if we don’t know who it belongs to.”
It wasn’t a confession or a promise, but there was something in the idea that had pierced through him, like a crack where something new had begun slipping inside, something that left him exposed.
She hadn’t written again since then, and he didn’t want to pressure her, but he didn’t want to remain silent either. Something was growing inside him, something difficult to name but impossible to ignore, the way you recognize a scent without knowing where it comes from and still feel as though it belongs to you. He opened a new file, not so much to write as to avoid seeing himself reflected in the dark screen. The cursor blinked insistently while he rested his fingers on the keyboard with that mixture of impulse and vertigo that appears when whatever is about to come out has no filter left.
At first there were scattered thoughts, then words arranging themselves without asking permission. He didn’t search for structure or technique, only truth.
I think about you quietly,
the way people think about someone they don’t want to lose.
I say your name in silence,
even when I don’t say it at all.
There are days when you don’t speak to me,
and somehow, you’re still here.
Like air inside a closed room,
like a photograph tucked away that never disappears even after nobody looks at it anymore.
You’re what appears when nothing should be there,
that pause that somehow doesn’t hurt.
I don’t know if this is love,
but if it isn’t,
then I don’t want to know what is.
He read it several times without correcting anything, because that was exactly what he carried inside himself. When he finally pressed “Send,” he felt the weight of the gesture, as though he had thrown something into the void without knowing whether anyone would find it, but understanding that keeping it to himself would have been worse. He stood up, stretched his arms, and walked toward the window. Buenos Aires was quiet: a taxi moved slowly down the street, a neighbor smoked on the balcony across from his building, and somewhere far away a dog barked without conviction. He rested his forehead against the glass, closed his eyes, and waited, not so much for an answer as for a breath of relief.
On the other side of the river, in Montevideo, Janet woke up for no clear reason, as though something had called her from far away. The room was dark except for the glow of her phone vibrating softly on the nightstand. She sat up slowly, still halfway between sleep and wakefulness, and picked it up. She saw his name.
Carlos.
She opened the message and began reading without rushing, stopping at each line as though she needed time to process what it was making her feel.
When she finished, no concrete thought came to her; she simply felt her throat tighten and tears begin to appear soundlessly. It wasn’t violent crying, but something deeper and contained, as though she were finally releasing something she had kept buried for far too long. She remained there motionless, holding the phone in both hands, until she finally managed to write a short reply loaded with everything that no longer needed explanation.
“I’m crying.
But this time, not because I’m afraid.”
She sent it without adding anything else and left the phone beside her, as though that alone were enough to keep her from feeling alone. Then she lay back down and, for the first time in days, closed her eyes without anguish, not because everything was suddenly okay, but because something inside her had finally been understood. And sometimes, that’s enough.
Carlos read the message an hour later. He didn’t reply. He simply rested the phone against his chest, closed his eyes, and let the smile appear on its own, effortlessly. It still wasn’t a love story, and it didn’t need to be yet; it was something simpler and, at the same time, more important: two people beginning to hold each other together without ever touching. And that, even if it was only beginning, was already enough to call it a beginning.







Chapter 7 – Where She Doesn’t Say “I Love You,” But Almost
Carlos read her reply and didn’t answer right away, not because he doubted what he wanted to say, but because he wanted to remain inside that moment a little longer, to hold it without touching it, as though any movement might break something that was quietly working on its own. It wasn’t only what she had said, but the way she had said it, that way of naming something without fully naming it, leaving everything exposed without making any of it explicit.
There was no “I love you,” but there didn’t need to be. In those few words there was something deeper, as though she were telling him that she recognized him, that a part of her had already placed him somewhere not everyone is allowed to enter, as though his words had been waiting to find her all along. And for Carlos, that was enough.
He rested the phone against his chest and lay back on the bed, feeling the world continue moving outside — the endless traffic, some neighbor dragging furniture across the floor, the fan humming with that tired mechanical sound — while inside him everything slowly became still. It wasn’t happiness in the traditional sense, not euphoria or relief, but something else, something more precise: recognition.
As though some part of him, the part that had always remained alone even while surrounded by people, had finally been seen without needing to explain itself, without being judged, simply accepted. And that carried a different kind of weight, one that didn’t need exaggeration to feel real.
In Montevideo, Janet was still sitting on the studio floor, her feet cold, the forgotten paintbrush resting between her fingers, the open window letting in air that didn’t really change anything. She looked at the city through a narrow opening in the curtains without waiting for answers, because in truth she didn’t need them; the only thing she wanted was for whatever this feeling was not to become another passing illusion.
The message had already been sent and she felt no need to reread it or correct it. She knew what she had said, even without using the obvious words. There had been something clear in that gesture: he was already inside her life somehow, she recognized him, and part of her had been waiting for him too without realizing it.
Neither of them wrote again that night, but the silence was no longer the same. It wasn’t uncertainty or distance anymore, but a shared space, something that had shifted quietly without needing noise or promises, held together only by soft words that, instead of disappearing, seemed determined to stay. And sometimes, that is enough.







Chapter 8 – The Voice Behind Everything
Carlos had spent more than twenty minutes writing and deleting the same sentence, a simple question that somehow carried far more weight than he expected. Every time he finished typing it and prepared to send it, something tightened inside his chest and stopped his hand, as though he were about to cross an invisible line. Even so, in one brief moment of determination, he took a deep breath and sent it before doubt had time to return:
“Would you be willing to do a call? Just voice. No camera.”
He left the phone on the bed and began pacing around the room with that physical restlessness that appears when waiting becomes impossible to endure sitting still. A few minutes passed that felt much longer than they really were, until the screen lit up with her reply:
“Yes. But not right now. Twenty minutes from now?”
Carlos answered with a simple thumbs-up, even though what he truly wanted to say could never have fit inside a gesture.
When the phone finally rang, he answered almost immediately, his voice low, as though the moment itself required gentleness.
“Hello?”
There was a brief silence, barely a second, and then Janet’s voice arrived, real for the first time, without screens or text between them.
“Hi, Carlos…”
It wasn’t the way he had imagined it. It was more concrete somehow, more human, carrying a faint exhaustion that made her feel even closer. And at the same time there was a tenderness in it capable of dismantling every defense he had left. Carlos closed his eyes without realizing it; he didn’t need to see her. Hearing her was enough.
“You sound… different,” he said, without really thinking about the words before speaking them.
She let out a soft laugh he couldn’t see but somehow still felt.
“Different compared to what?”
“To everything.”
The silence that followed wasn’t uncomfortable, only dense, as though both of them were slowly adjusting to this new space between them.
“I wanted to hear you,” he added. “Not out of curiosity… out of necessity.”
“I was scared too,” Janet admitted softly. “Because once you hear someone’s voice… you stop imagining.”
Carlos hesitated for a second before asking:
“And are you disappointed?”
“No,” she answered. “I’m… calmer.”
Something inside him loosened at that exact moment, as though only then had he stopped bracing himself for impact.
“Do you want me to read you the poem?”
Janet took a few seconds before answering, as though weighing what it meant to say yes.
“Yes… please.”
Carlos had a recorded version of it saved, but he didn’t use it. Instead, he opened the file and read it aloud right there, his voice trembling slightly, making no effort to hide the emotion behind it. When he finished, there was no immediate reaction, no quick comment waiting on the other side; only a silence stretching long enough to say something on its own, followed finally by a soft sigh.
“Thank you,” she whispered.
Carlos ran a hand over his face, still carrying the feeling that he had exposed something far bigger than a simple piece of writing.
“I like your voice,” Janet added.
“Why?”
“Because it doesn’t sound like it’s trying to convince me of anything. It’s just… there. And it sounds like it’s scared too.”
Carlos let out a quiet breath, almost a brief laugh.
“It is.”
And inside that simple exchange, without declarations or promises, both of them understood that something was beginning to take shape. There was still no need to define it; it was enough to know it was being built from an honest place, without disguises, sustained only by the truth they were slowly daring to share with each other.





Chapter 9 – The Color of Love in Silence
She couldn’t sleep. It wasn’t exactly insomnia, but something deeper, more restless than that. Janet had gone to bed early, yet her body refused to surrender to rest; every time she closed her eyes, Carlos’s voice returned to her, not his messages or his pictures, but that trembling voice reading the poem aloud, so real it seemed to linger in the air long after the call had ended. She didn’t want it to disappear.
She got up without turning on the light and slipped into the worn linen robe she only used when painting late at night, as though that small gesture opened some intimate ritual inside her. She walked quietly toward the studio, feeling the wooden floor creak beneath her bare feet, surrounded once again by that dense scent of oil paint and nighttime that always seemed to wrap itself around her. A blank canvas waited in the corner; she hadn’t intended to use it, but something inside her was moving too strongly now to ignore.
She turned on the old radio resting on the shelf and the sound came through muffled, tangled with static. Then, emerging from the interference, came the voice of Mercedes Sosa, deep and aching, filling the room with Alfonsina y el mar. Janet closed her eyes and felt something inside her open completely.
She picked up a brush without sketching first, without thinking, and began to paint. At first the strokes were firm, almost defensive, but slowly they became looser, more honest. A deep wine-red spread across the background like a wound that no longer bleeds but still pulses underneath the skin, and over that color shapes began to emerge: two figures barely suggested, separated by only the smallest distance, almost touching. One of them seemed to be looking; the other had no face at all, only a faint outline, as though it existed solely because someone else was seeing it.
She painted for an hour, maybe two; time stopped carrying meaning inside that room. When she finished, she remained standing in front of the canvas without judging it, simply feeling that she had finally said something she had carried silently for too long. Chapter 16 – When the Air Starts to Feel Heavy

What if I ruin this?

Chapter 22 – When Staying Hurts Too

Carlos.

Chapter 24 – What I Keep to Myself So I Don’t Break You

Chapter 26 – The Gallery Without Her

Janet.
Montevideo.
Oil painting.

Carlos.

Chapter 27 – Screaming Inside While Staying Silent

Carlos.

she needed him more than ever.

Not from guilt.
Not from collapse.
Not from a darkness she still didn’t know how to share with another person.

Chapter 29 – The Moment Everything Starts to Cool

Chapter 31 – “I’m Coming With You”

Not because I’m afraid.

I just wanted you to know.

Then I’m coming with you.

Because there was no going back from this decision anymore.

Chapter 32 – The Country Falls Apart, and Love Arrives Too Late

It came apart slowly, like a chain losing tension link by link until it finally gave way completely. And even though that morning’s headlines still tried to soften reality with careful language, out in the streets no explanation was necessary anymore because everything was happening at the same time: looting in neighborhood stores, metal shutters slamming down like frightened eyelids, sirens overlapping endlessly without pause, helicopters flying low as though searching for something they could no longer control, crowds running without fully knowing what they were escaping from, and police alongside military forces occupying entire intersections with shields, helmets, and a tension so thick it could be felt without anyone needing to look directly at them.

The only thing that still made sense was movement.

Janet sitting alone at the airport beside her suitcase,

watching the entrance,

waiting.

Signal.

Messages.

Anything.

Any sign that he was close.

The clock kept advancing anyway.

He never came.

But he wasn’t there.

There were no screams.

No dramatic breakdown.

Janet’s last message.

You’re not here.

I’m leaving anyway.

and still not enough.

Because this had never been about lack of love.

Or hesitation.

Or fear.

It was simply a moment that reality itself refused to allow.

But for Carlos, time had stopped completely at that exact point where love hadn’t ended, hadn’t disappeared, hadn’t even failed.

It had simply arrived too late.

And somehow that hurt more than any clear ending ever could.

Because it left behind a possibility that had been real…

but never got the chance to exist.

every sale,
every applause,
every recognition,

she was okay.

Chapter 35 – Hope Is a Clock That Never Tells the Exact Time

Someone dependable.

Janet.

Her voice during those late-night calls.

Her paintings carrying emotions that never fully explained themselves.

Her restrained laugh.

The message that arrived too late.

The waiting that collapsed quietly between them.

You still appear when I’m not even thinking about you,

Carlos had never considered himself a nostalgic person.

he never forgot her.

Someone who had found balance.

Something small.

Persistent.

Chapter 37 – The Love You Don’t Touch So It Won’t Break Again

“Someone asked about you.

It wasn’t shock.

Carlos.

“And that’s what hurts the most.”







Chapter 38 – The Answer That Cannot Be Replied To

Mrs. Sadness.

Someone who loved me enough to know how to leave without breaking me further.

Mrs. Sadness.

It was her.

At the same moment another mirrored truth settled quietly inside him: he had painted her too, even without canvases, through every text never sent, every sentence written and erased, every silence he chose to preserve instead of filling with words that would have arrived too late.

The song continued softly in the background, accompanying the recognition without interrupting it, and when the voice repeated that time may say goodbye without erasing what remains carried inside, Carlos closed his eyes for barely a second, just long enough to feel that he was not remembering something lost, but touching something still alive in another form.

The painting did not look directly at him. It had no face, no name, no explicit acknowledgment, and yet it embraced him with a precision no literal portrait ever could have achieved, because hidden inside that tiny detail — that concealed hummingbird — existed a continuity that required no explanation.

He remained there several minutes longer without moving much, without needing to do anything at all, because the moment demanded not action but presence. He walked back toward the street carrying the same cold coffee and the same headphones around his ears, yet the world no longer felt entirely identical to the one he had entered from earlier, and what he carried inside his chest was neither nostalgia nor pain, but a calm certainty — the kind that makes no noise and yet quietly rearranges everything.

She still carried him, not through speeches or declarations, but through details no one else would ever recognize.

And he carried her too, not as an absence heavy enough to wound him anymore, but as a way of having loved that no longer had any possibility of disappearing.

Because there are loves that do not need to remain physically present in order to stay alive, and when they are real, they find ways to continue existing in places no one else notices — places where both people, even from far away, still know exactly what they are looking at.








Chapter 43 – The Story That Could Not Be Lived, But Could Be Told

The story no longer belonged only to her or to him. It belonged to what it had meant. To what it had taught. To what it had left behind.

And there, in that stillness, without needing to say it aloud, he finally understood something that years earlier would have wounded him to accept and which now simply existed, clear and weightless:

that some forms of love do not need to touch in order to be real,

and that sometimes the most honest thing is not insisting that something continue forever,

but allowing it to remain exactly where it was once true, without distorting it, without demanding from it more than it was ever able to give.

Chapter 45 – The Name That Carries a Story

I kept loving her… even after she no longer had her name.
By the time the song reached its highest point, Carlos was already disconnecting from the moment, removing the microphone with a simple gesture, saying goodbye without ceremony, and walking out of the room into an evening where the sunlight was beginning to fade and the air carried that coolness announcing the end of the day without turning it into something dramatic.
And in that transition from a controlled space into a real one, he understood something that no longer required confirmation: that there are loves that do not need physical presence in order to endure, that do not require promises in order to persist, and that can continue existing within a dimension untouched by reunions or farewells.
Because there are stories that never begin again, but never entirely end either, and within that silent balance, far away from what is visible, they find a way to remain forever.



Chapter 50 – Final Poem: To You, Always You

I never got married.
Not because of fear.
Not because of pain.
Because of certainty.

Because from that very first message of yours,
without flowers or fire,
I knew that place inside me
was already taken.

I never forgot you.
Not because I couldn’t,
but because you were there
in everything that was true.

In the songs I never finished.
In sunsets that lingered for no reason.
In books…
that, without meaning to, spoke your name.

You were my place in a world
that never knew how to hold me.

My poem without metaphor.
My waiting without time.
My silence… without cold.

It was you.

Even if life separated us
through everything visible:
cities, decisions, fears…

What I felt
didn’t leave.
Didn’t wear away.
Didn’t hide.

It stayed.

And it still does.

Because what we had
had no body…
but it had everything else.

And that
doesn’t end.

If one day someone asks me
why I never searched for another story,
why I never filled my life with other names…

I will answer, without sadness:

“Because it already happened to me.
Because I already loved her.”

And there is no repetition for that.

To you,
who didn’t stay…
but never truly left.

Thank you.

For who you were.
For who you are.
And for who, inside me,
you will continue to be.

THE END

Carlos Rubino
(or perhaps… Mr. Sadness)
